Terrain data can describe more than height. A moisture map, a material map, or a distance-to-water function can describe the conditions at each candidate position. The scattering system reads those conditions before deciding whether to place an object.

**Farmland** shows two ideas at once. First, a field covers an area, so random points would leave gaps and overlaps. Its candidates come from a **lattice**: one candidate at the centre of every cell of a regular grid, so accepted cells sit side by side as tiles. Random candidates suit rocks; a lattice suits objects that must fit together.

Second, a field needs water. In this designed world, a tile is accepted when its **distance to water** is inside an irrigation band *and* its height is above the water level. Both conditions must hold. A distance band on its own would let "near water" quietly mean "inside the water".

A map can be a stored grid or a function asked for a value at a position. A noise-based moisture map is a useful artistic control, but it is not a physical simulation of moisture, and it should not be presented as one. Slope comes later: for now a tile on a hillside is still accepted.

The farming rules in this lesson are rules of a designed world, not claims about real agriculture.
