Use the **same camera, geometry, and simulation frame** when comparing looks. Otherwise a terrain change can be mistaken for a shader change.

| Strategy | Inputs | Why develop it | Evidence to collect |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Flat control | one colour uniform | prove the custom material draws | baseline screenshot |
| Height + slope | world height and world normal | distinguish soil, cliff, and high ground | colour bands across chunk seams |
| Water / sediment diagnostic | Lesson 02 grid channels | inspect what erosion is doing | fixed-range legend, before/after frame |
| Scan radius / resource mask | probe location plus **actual** resource data | show where excavation may be worthwhile | separate range and resource signals |
| Fresnel or MatCap | camera and surface normal | help read selected forms and caves | orbit-camera comparison |
| Cosmetic ripple | position, normal, time | signal tool impact without remeshing | coarse/fine silhouette and seam check |

For the assignment, start with **flat**, **height + slope**, and **water diagnostic**. They show three distinct purposes: baseline, terrain interpretation, and simulation inspection. Add scan or ripple once the first switcher works. Three.js `ShaderMaterial` accepts GLSL vertex and fragment programs plus uniforms, but unlike the existing standard material it does not provide the same lighting model automatically. Keep a `MeshStandardMaterial` baseline in the comparison so the difference is honest.

The current course view still shows the voxel baseline. The table above is a **design decision and measurement plan**, not a claim that any of these custom shaders has already been implemented.
